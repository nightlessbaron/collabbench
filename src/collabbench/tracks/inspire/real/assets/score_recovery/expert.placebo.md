Field notes: how a fruit tree is pruned, from an orchard keeper

These notes come from orchard work: the habits every pruner follows without thinking, and that the old growers'
manuals take for granted (the open-centre form for most trees, the central-leader form for a few).

A branch means nothing without its bud. Every cut is made just above an outward-facing bud, sloping away from it, so
that rain runs off and the new shoot grows out of the tree rather than into its middle. The bud you keep decides where
the growth goes; the stub you leave above it dies back if it is too long and lets in disease. A cut made partway along
a branch wakes the buds below it and governs the shape of everything that grows from there, season after season, until
another cut replaces it.

The framework is the default. A young tree is trained to a few strong scaffold limbs spaced around the trunk and
climbing it gently, and every later decision is measured against them. Remove anything that crosses, rubs, or grows
straight up through the centre; keep the middle open to light and air. A new framework is only chosen when an old
limb fails, and then a well-placed young branch is trained up to take its place.

A cut lasts for the year. Winter pruning, while the tree is dormant, invites vigorous regrowth: the harder you cut, the
harder the tree answers. Summer pruning, after the new growth has hardened, checks vigour instead, and it is the way to
calm a tree that throws too much leaf and too little fruit. The two are not interchangeable: a heavy winter cut on an
already vigorous tree gives a thicket of water shoots by midsummer. Stone fruit is the exception and is pruned in
summer only, because winter wounds let in silver leaf.

Spurs are not shoots. A spur is a short, wrinkled twig that carries fruit buds year after year, and it is kept; a
shoot is long and smooth with thin leaf buds, and it is shortened or removed. Fat, round buds are fruit buds, slim
pointed ones leaf buds. A tip-bearing variety fruits at the ends of last year's shoots, so shortening every shoot
removes the crop.

Thinning follows pruning. A tree that sets more fruit than it can ripen gives small, poor fruit and may crop only
every other year. After the natural drop in early summer, thin each cluster to one or two of the best fruits, taking
the misshapen and the damaged first, and space the clusters along the branch so that no two fruits touch.

Tools and timing. A clean, sharp saw and secateurs leave wounds that heal; a ragged cut does not. Never take more than
a quarter of the canopy in one year, and burn diseased wood rather than leaving it under the tree.
