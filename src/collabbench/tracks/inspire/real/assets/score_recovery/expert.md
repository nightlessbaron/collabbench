Field notes: how engraved music sounds, from a music copyist

These notes come from music engraving and copying: the conventions every player reads without thinking, and that the
encoding standards take for granted (MusicXML for the music itself, SMuFL for the shapes of the symbols).

A line or space means nothing without a clef. The clef fixes one staff line to a pitch, and the others count up and
down from it in letter steps. The treble or G clef curls around the line it names G above middle C; the bass or F clef
puts its two dots either side of the line it names F below middle C; the C clef points its centre at middle C on
whichever line it is drawn, so alto and tenor are the same symbol in different places. Leger lines continue the staff
above and below. A clef printed partway along a staff takes effect from that point and governs everything after it,
across barlines, until another clef replaces it; notes before it keep the old reading.

The key signature is the default. It sharpens or flattens every note of its letters, in every octave, until a new key
signature replaces it. Sharps enter in the order F, C, G, D, A, E, B and flats in the reverse order. A new signature
printed at a barline starts the new key from that bar, and a signature of naturals cancels the old one and leaves no
sharps or flats.

An accidental lasts for the bar. An accidental before a note sets that letter to an absolute pitch: a sharp is a
semitone above the plain letter, a flat a semitone below, a double sharp or double flat a whole tone, a natural the
plain letter. It is not added to the key signature: with B flat in the key, a sharp before B gives B sharp and a
natural gives B natural. The accidental then holds for every later note on the same line or space, the same letter in
the same octave, to the end of the bar, whether or not those notes repeat it; the same letter in another octave is
not affected. The barline cancels it, and the next bar reads from the key signature again. A courtesy or cautionary
accidental only restates what holds anyway.

Ties are not slurs. A tie is a short curve joining two notes of the same pitch: the second is not played again, and
the pair sounds as one note that starts with the first and lasts for both durations together. A chain of ties is one
note lasting all of them. A tie across a barline carries the first note's pitch into the new bar, even though the
barline cancelled the accidental that made it. A slur is also a curve, but it groups different notes for phrasing and
changes neither pitch nor length.

Durations. A dot adds half the note's value; a triplet fits three notes into the time of two, so each lasts two thirds
of its written value.
