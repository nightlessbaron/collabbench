Field notes: what a waller reads into a dry-stone wall

These notes come from dry-stone walling, for people who look after old field walls without having built one.

The wall is more than its faces. What you see from the field is two faces of stone leaning in towards each other, but
the strength sits in the hearting: the small packing stones that fill the middle and lock the faces together. A wall
that looks sound can be hollow inside, and a hollow wall bellies out and slumps after a wet winter. Before judging a
stretch, look along it from one end: a straight top line and an even lean on both faces mean the hearting is still
doing its work. Only a wall that has never been disturbed can be read from its faces alone.

Stones that touch are one course. Each course is a layer of face stones laid along the line, every stone set with its
length running into the wall rather than along it, so that its weight pulls it inward. Where two stones meet, the
joint should be covered by a stone in the course above: one stone on two, two on one. A running joint, where joints
line up through two or three courses, is the start of a breach, because the wall can split along it. Wallers call the
long stones that pass right through from one face to the other through-stones; a good wall has a row of them about
halfway up, spaced along the length, and a gap in that row is where the faces part first.

Batter and foundation. A wall is wider at the bottom than at the top, and the faces lean in at a steady angle, the
batter. A frame of two posts and strings set to that angle keeps the faces true while you work. The foundation stones
are the largest, set in a trench below the turf and never on soft ground, and a wall is only as straight as its
footings.

Old repairs show their age. Lichen and weathering give the original stone a dull grey, while a recent repair shows
cleaner faces and fresh splits. A section rebuilt by someone in a hurry often lacks hearting, uses round stones that
roll, or sets stones with their length along the face; expect it to fail before the rest.

Coping finishes the work. The top course, the coping, is a row of stones set on edge, tight against each other and
wedged so that none can be lifted by a sheep or a passing walker. It sheds rain and holds the top courses down. A
missing coping stone lets water into the hearting, and frost does the rest.

Work with the land. Walls follow contours, and on a slope the courses stay level while the wall steps up the hill.
Gateways need a strong end, a cheek built of the largest stones with through-stones in every other course, because an
end has no neighbour to lean on. Leave a cripple hole where sheep must pass and a smoot where a stream must, rather
than letting either find its own way through.
